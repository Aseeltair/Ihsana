How we rank priorities

A transparent account of how Ihsana turns country data into a short list of solvable priorities — and what the model does not claim to do.

Purpose

The Priorities Dashboard exists to answer a practical question: where can a donor’s next commitment do the most measurable good? It is not a ranking of need alone, and it is not a ranking of charity quality. It is a starting point for a country, a problem and a conversation.

The model narrows a long list of development indicators into three priorities per country. It favours problems that are large, urgent, solvable with known interventions, and neglected relative to their burden.

The output is a short list, not a verdict. Every priority still requires diligence on the partner, the delivery model and the local context.

What the model measures

Each country is described by three elements: the scale of preventable suffering, the existence of proven interventions, and the country’s capacity to deliver them. The model then groups specific needs under the five Maqasid:

The five Maqasid and the needs the model groups under them
MaqsadTypical needs in the model
Life · al-NafsMaternal survival, newborn survival, enteric infections, malaria and tropical disease
Progeny · al-NaslChildhood nutrition, maternal and child health, family stability
Intellect · al-AqlChildren out of school, learning quality, skills and training
Wealth · al-MalYouth employment, women’s economic participation, financial inclusion
Faith · al-DinReligious life, community institutions, civil and religious rights

Where a country has a distinctive burden — a collapsed health system, a displacement crisis, a very young labour market — the model allows that context to change which priority rises to the top. The framework is consistent; the answer is country-specific.

Where the data comes from

The published model draws on international health, education and economic datasets, then applies Ihsana’s analytical judgements. The preview uses a curated 27-country subset so the interactions can be tested without publishing the full private dataset.

For a production release, the team will document each indicator, its source, its year and any transformation applied. A donor should be able to trace a priority back to the evidence behind it.

How a country is ranked

The country rank is a composite. It combines the scale of preventable need with the degree to which the need is solvable and the presence of delivery constraints. A country can rise in the ranking because its burden is large, because its burden is unusually solvable, or because both are true.

The four tests

  1. Burden. How many people are affected, and how severe is the consequence?
  2. Solvability. Is there a proven intervention, and what does it cost per outcome?
  3. Readiness. Can a credible partner deliver at scale in the current context?
  4. Neglect. Is the need underfunded relative to its share of the burden?

The rank is a prompt for diligence, not a substitute for it. Two countries with similar scores can require very different giving strategies.

How a priority is chosen

Within each country, needs are grouped into candidate priorities. A candidate rises when it is large, tractable and underweighted. It falls when the binding constraint is political, when no credible delivery route exists, or when the evidence for the intervention is weak.

Each priority card in the dashboard therefore answers three questions: what is the need, why is it solvable, and what would a donor actually fund? Where the model has a headline figure, it is shown; where it does not, the card describes the need without inventing a number.

Read the five priority cards as a portfolio of problems, not as a menu of charities. The next step is to match a priority to a partner with a delivery model that can be measured.

What the status labels mean

Every country carries one status label. The label describes the giving stance the model recommends, not the worth of the people who live there.

Status labels used in the dashboard
StatusWhat it means for a donor
FundUrgent, solvable needs where additional funding can change outcomes now.

MaintainProgress is holding; protect gains and improve delivery rather than scale.
DiasporaMuslim communities in Western countries with distinct inclusion priorities.

Limits and caveats

  • The model cannot capture every local reality. It is a national lens, and needs vary within countries.
  • Rankings are sensitive to data quality and year. A change in the source data can move a country.
  • Solvability is not the same as political feasibility. A proven intervention can still be blocked.
  • The dashboard does not vet individual charities. Partner diligence remains a separate step.
  • Figures marked as illustrative in this preview must not be cited as published Ihsana data.

How to use the dashboard

Start with a country that matters to you, or filter by status to see where additional funding is most likely to change outcomes. Read the three priorities. Then ask three questions before committing:

  1. Which Maqsad does this protect, and how would we know it worked?
  2. What is the binding constraint, and does the proposed partner address it?
  3. What would we stop funding to make this commitment serious?

Those questions are the bridge between a dashboard and a giving strategy. If you want help answering them, get in touch.

Review and updates

The model is reviewed as new data becomes available. In production, the team updates the underlying data monthly and records the date of the last review on the dashboard. Changes to the ranking method will be versioned so that older analyses remain intelligible.
